# 1. Given the area of a hexagon, find its side length

## Setup

A regular hexagon splits into 6 equilateral triangles of side $s$.

Area of one equilateral triangle:

$$
A_\triangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2
$$

So the hexagon area is:

$$
A = 6 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2 = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}s^2
$$

## Answer

Solving for $s$:

$$
\boxed{
s = \sqrt{\frac{2A}{3\sqrt{3}}}
}
$$

## Worked example

If $A = 54\sqrt{3}$:

$$
s^2 = \frac{2\left(54\sqrt{3}\right)}{3\sqrt{3}} = \frac{108\sqrt{3}}{3\sqrt{3}} = 36
$$

$$
\boxed{s = 6}
$$

## Sanity check

Area scales as $s^2$, so doubling the side quadruples the area — consistent with the square root in the formula.

## What to say aloud

> A regular hexagon is six equilateral triangles. I use the equilateral-triangle area formula, multiply by six, then invert to solve for the side length.

# 2. Why is summer hotter than winter?

## Answer

**Earth's axial tilt (~23.5°), not distance from the Sun.**

The tilt changes the **angle of incidence** of sunlight, which changes energy delivered per unit ground area, and it changes **day length**, which changes total daily energy.

## The incidence-angle math

Let $\theta$ be the angle between incoming sunlight and the surface normal.

A beam of cross-sectional area $A_\text{beam}$ spreads over ground area:

$$
A_\text{ground} = \frac{A_\text{beam}}{\cos\theta}
$$

So intensity per unit ground area is:

$$
\boxed{I = I_0 \cos\theta}
$$

| Sun angle from vertical $\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ | Relative heating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0^\circ$ (overhead) | 1.00 | 100% |
| $30^\circ$ | 0.87 | 87% |
| $60^\circ$ | 0.50 | 50% |
| $75^\circ$ | 0.26 | 26% |

At 60° off vertical, the same sunlight is smeared over **twice** the ground area — half the heating.

## The two compounding effects

| Effect | Summer | Winter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incidence angle | Near-vertical → high $\cos\theta$ | Shallow → low $\cos\theta$ |
| Day length | Longer (more hours of input) | Shorter |
| Atmospheric path | Shorter (less scattering/absorption) | Longer |

Total daily energy $\approx$ intensity $\times$ duration, so both factors push the same direction — the effect is multiplicative, not additive.

## The trap to avoid

Earth is at **perihelion (closest to the Sun) in early January** — during Northern Hemisphere winter. Distance varies only ~3%, and it works *against* the naive "closer = hotter" explanation. If distance were the driver, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time; they don't.

## What to say aloud

> It's the axial tilt, not distance. Tilt changes the incidence angle, and intensity scales with the cosine of that angle — at 60° off vertical you get half the energy per square meter. Longer summer days compound it. The giveaway that distance isn't the cause is that the hemispheres have opposite seasons, and Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.

# 5. Drop a dense stone from a floating boat into the water

Assume:

$$
\rho*{\text{stone}} > \rho*{\text{water}}
$$

so the stone sinks.

Question:

Does the swimming-pool water level rise or fall?

$$
\boxed{\text{It falls}}
$$

---

## Before the stone is dropped

While the stone is inside the floating boat, the boat must displace water equal to the **stone's weight**.

If stone mass is $m$, displaced water volume attributable to the stone is:

$$
V\_{\text{before}}
=
\frac{m}{\rho_w}
$$

---

## After the stone sinks

The stone displaces only its own physical volume:

$$
V\_{\text{after}}
=
\frac{m}{\rho_s}
$$

Because:

$$
\rho_s > \rho_w
$$

we have:

$$
\frac{m}{\rho_s}

<
\frac{m}{\rho_w}
$$

Therefore:

$$
V*{\text{after}} < V*{\text{before}}
$$

and the water level:

$$
\boxed{\text{falls}}
$$

## What to say aloud

> In the boat, the stone causes displacement equal to its weight. Once submerged, it displaces only its volume. Because the stone is denser than water, that physical volume is smaller than the equivalent-weight volume of water.

# 8. Probability that the 13th day of a month is Friday

For a simple interview model, if weekdays are approximately uniformly distributed:

$$
\boxed{\frac17}
$$

But over the real Gregorian calendar, weekday frequencies are **not perfectly uniform**.

The Gregorian calendar repeats every:

$$
400 \text{ years}
$$

Number of months:

$$
400\times12=4800
$$

The exact probability that the 13th falls on Friday is:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{688}{4800}
=
\frac{43}{300}
\approx 14.333\%
}
$$

Compare:

$$
\frac17\approx14.286\%
$$

So Friday is slightly more common than perfect uniformity would imply.

## About “Fermat's Little Theorem”

Fermat's Little Theorem is not normally the key theorem for solving this problem.

The useful ideas are:

- arithmetic modulo 7;
- leap-year rules;
- the Gregorian calendar's 400-year repetition.

## Interview answer

If no calendar model is specified:

> Assuming the weekday of the 13th is uniform, $1/7$. If you mean the exact Gregorian-calendar frequency, it is $688/4800 = 43/300$.

# 10. Distance from a point to a plane in 3D

Plane:

$$
Ax+By+Cz+D=0
$$

Point:

$$
P=(x_0,y_0,z_0)
$$

Distance:

$$
\boxed{
d=
\frac{
|Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|
}{
\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}
}
}
$$

---

## Why?

The plane normal vector is:

$$
\vec n=(A,B,C)
$$

Choose any point $Q$ on the plane.

The perpendicular distance is the magnitude of the projection of:

$$
P-Q
$$

onto the unit normal.

Thus:

$$
d
=
\left|
(P-Q)\cdot
\frac{\vec n}{\|\vec n\|}
\right|
$$

which simplifies to the formula above.

# 12. Expected number of times `max` is updated

Suppose we scan a random permutation of $n$ distinct values.

Pseudo-code:

```ts
let max = -Infinity;

for (const x of values) {
  if (x > max) {
    max = x;
  }
}
```

Question:

How many times do we expect `max` to change?

---

## Key observation

At position $i$, the element is a new maximum exactly when it is the largest among the first $i$ elements.

Every one of those $i$ positions is equally likely to contain the largest value.

Therefore:

$$
P(\text{update at }i)=\frac1i
$$

Define indicator:

$$
X_i=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if position }i\text{ sets a new max}\\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

Then:

$$
X=\sum\_{i=1}^nX_i
$$

By linearity of expectation:

$$
E[X]
=
\sum\_{i=1}^nE[X_i]
$$

# $$

\sum\_{i=1}^n\frac1i
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
E[X]=H_n
}
$$

where $H_n$ is the $n$-th harmonic number.

Approximation:

$$
H_n
\approx
\ln n+\gamma
$$

where:

$$
\gamma\approx0.57721
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
E[X]\approx\ln n+\gamma
}
$$

## Example

For:

$$
n=1{,}000{,}000
$$

$$
E[X]
\approx
\ln(10^6)+0.577
$$

$$
\approx13.815+0.577
$$

$$
\boxed{\approx14.4}
$$

Interesting result:

Even with one million values, the maximum changes only about 14 times on average.

# $$

\frac{L}{D}\sin\theta

$$

\frac{2}{\pi}

\frac{2L}{\pi D}
[-\cos\theta]\_0^{\pi/2}

\boxed{

}

# 14. Match a small 2D point set inside a larger point set

You have:

- source / template set $S$, small;
- target set $T$, large;
- allowed transformation: **rotation + translation**;
- no scaling.

Goal:

Find the transformation producing the best alignment.

This is a form of:

- rigid point-set registration;
- geometric matching;
- Procrustes alignment / ICP, depending on correspondence assumptions.

---

## Case A — Point correspondences are known

Suppose source points $p_i$ correspond to target points $q_i$.

We want:

$$
q_i \approx Rp_i+t
$$

where:

- $R$ is a 2D rotation matrix;
- $t$ is a translation.

### Step 1 — Compute centroids

$$
\bar p
=
\frac1n\sum p_i
$$

$$
\bar q
=
\frac1n\sum q_i
$$

### Step 2 — Center the points

$$
p_i'=p_i-\bar p
$$

$$
q_i'=q_i-\bar q
$$

### Step 3 — Solve rotation

Minimize:

$$
\sum_i
\|q_i'-Rp_i'\|^2
$$

Use SVD / Kabsch algorithm.

For matrix:

$$
H=\sum_i p_i' q_i'^T
$$

compute:

$$
H=U\Sigma V^T
$$

then:

$$
R=VU^T
$$

with determinant correction if necessary.

### Step 4 — Translation

$$
t=\bar q-R\bar p
$$

Complexity:

$$
\boxed{O(n)}
$$

aside from constant-size SVD.

---

## Case B — Correspondences are unknown

Now the harder problem is finding which target points correspond to source points.

Possible approaches:

### Approach 1 — Anchor-pair hashing

Because rotation and translation preserve distances:

$$
\|p_i-p_j\|
=
\|q_a-q_b\|
$$

Use pairwise distances as rigid invariants.

Steps:

1. Compute characteristic distances in template.
2. Search target pairs with approximately matching distances.
3. Each matching pair defines a candidate rotation and translation.
4. Transform all template points.
5. Use a spatial index to score how many transformed points match target points.

Use:

- KD-tree;
- spatial hash;
- R-tree.

Candidate verification:

$$
O(m\log N)
$$

for template size $m$.

---

### Approach 2 — RANSAC

Useful with noise and outliers.

1. Pick a minimal sample from template.
2. Pick candidate points from target.
3. Infer rigid transform.
4. Count inliers.
5. Repeat.
6. Refine best transform.

Pros:

- robust to outliers;
- easy to explain.

Cons:

- probabilistic;
- can require many iterations.

---

### Approach 3 — ICP

Iterative Closest Point:

1. Start from initial transform.
2. Match each source point to nearest target point.
3. solve optimal rigid transform;
4. repeat.

Pros:

- good local refinement.

Cons:

- local optimum;
- needs good initialization.

---

## Strong interview architecture

For a small template in a large dataset:

```text
Template
  ↓
Invariant features
(pairwise distances / angles)
  ↓
Candidate target anchors
  ↓
Infer R,t
  ↓
Transform template
  ↓
KD-tree nearest-neighbor scoring
  ↓
RANSAC / best score
  ↓
Optional ICP refinement
```

# 18. Find the pattern in the number of edges

The prompt references an image (`edges.img`) that is not included, so the exact intended sequence cannot be determined.

For visual pattern questions, use this framework.

---

## Step 1 — Create a sequence

Count edges in each figure:

$$
E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4,\dots
$$

---

## Step 2 — First differences

Compute:

$$
\Delta E*n=E*{n+1}-E_n
$$

If constant:

$$
E_n=an+b
$$

---

## Step 3 — Second differences

If first differences are not constant, compute:

$$
\Delta^2E_n
$$

Constant second differences suggest a quadratic:

$$
E_n=an^2+bn+c
$$

---

## Step 4 — Structural counting

Instead of relying only on numerical pattern matching, count geometrically:

$$
\boxed{
\text{new edges}
=
\text{edges added by new unit}

- \text{shared edges}
  }
  $$

For shapes made of repeated polygons:

$$
E*n
=
nE*{\text{shape}}

- E\_{\text{shared}}
  $$

Example: $n$ squares in a row.

One square has:

$$
4
$$

edges.

Each additional square shares one edge, so it contributes only:

$$
3
$$

new edges.

Therefore:

$$
E_n
=
4+3(n-1)
$$

$$
\boxed{
E_n=3n+1
}
$$

# Interview Strategy

For these questions, the interviewer is usually testing **reasoning more than memorization**.

Use this structure:

```text
1. Clarify assumptions.
2. Identify the physical / mathematical invariant.
3. Express it as a formula.
4. Solve symbolically before substituting numbers.
5. Check the units.
6. Check limiting cases.
7. Explain the intuition in one sentence.
```

## Examples of strong clarification

For video storage:

> Resolution and FPS aren't enough to determine storage if the video is compressed. Should I assume raw RGB or a target bitrate?

For circle coverage:

> Do you mean a true covering where circles may overlap, or densest packing of equal non-overlapping circles?

For tic-tac-toe:

> Since 49 is the expected answer, I'll assume a 3×3×3 board where three aligned cells win.

# Common Traps

### Trap 1 — Linear vs cubic scaling

A sphere 50% larger in diameter is **not** 50% heavier.

$$
1.5^3=3.375
$$

---

### Trap 2 — Ignoring missing information

1080p30 does not uniquely specify file size.

You need:

$$
\boxed{\text{bits per pixel or bitrate}}
$$

---

### Trap 3 — Confusing weight displacement with volume displacement

Stone in boat:

```text
boat → displacement proportional to stone weight
water → displacement equal to stone volume
```

---

### Trap 4 — Forgetting posterior probabilities

After seeing a 3, d6 and d8 are no longer equally probable.

---

### Trap 5 — Assuming a supplied answer is automatically correct

The standard Buffon's Needle answer for $L=D$ is:

$$
\boxed{2/\pi}
$$

not:

$$
1/\pi
$$
